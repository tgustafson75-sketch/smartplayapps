What it does today, and what it can’t do

Security products tend to promise more than they can enforce. We checked what Chrome actually allows an extension to do, and only list what holds up.

Working in the lab build
  • Scope alerts. Warns when a tab lands in a tab group that looks like an AI agent’s.
  • Sensitive-site alerts. Email, banking and health portals are on the list by default. If an agent may be on one, Defender tells you.
  • Read-only consent. You can give an agent 30 minutes of read-only access to one tab. Defender records it and lets it expire.
  • Setup scan. Lists your installed extensions and what each one is allowed to touch.
  • Activity log, on your device. What Defender saw and decided. Never what a page said.
What it can’t do
  • It can’t stop another AI agent from reading a page. Chrome gives one extension no way to block another’s reads.
  • It can’t prove which agent owns a tab group. So every alert says “possible”, never “confirmed”.
  • It can’t pause another agent’s clicks or sends. That switch is shown greyed out, with the reason, instead of pretending.

Checking what an email wants, not just who sent it

In November a fake job-offer email reached one of our inboxes. It passed every sender check. The domain was real, so the filters let it through. What gave it away was what it asked for.

Sender checks: all passed
SPFPASS
DKIMPASS
DMARCPASS
Spam scoreLOW

Passing these proves the email came from where it says. It says nothing about whether it’s safe.

What it was trying to do
  1. The display name didn’t match the address it came from.
  2. You weren’t in To or Cc. You were blind-copied, under a “noreply” address.
  3. It greeted someone else by name.
  4. It asked for your cell number, to move the conversation off email.
  5. It carried a web-page attachment.

Defender’s next step is to read these signals from what’s on screen and switch to Alert, still saying “possible”. First we have to confirm Chrome lets an extension read them in Gmail and Outlook.
